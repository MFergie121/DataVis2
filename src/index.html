Close but no Cigar

Victoria's hospitality crowds in tight against public transport. You'd expect that closeness to pay off. It doesn't: being near a stop won't keep a venue's doors open.

Max Fergie · FIT2179 Data Visualisation 2 · May 2026

Setup

Victoria's venues huddle against the transport network

Where the state drinks, dines and gathers, how short is the walk to a stop? Across all of Victoria, then zoomed in on Melbourne, the venues settle exactly where the network is densest. It's hard not to assume that closeness pays off. A tram at the door should mean passing trade, a steady flow of people who never had to find a park, more foot traffic for people to stumble into your venue.

One clarification first. Throughout, near a stop means proximity. As in how far you'd walk to board, not how often a service actually runs. Colour on these maps is distance, not frequency of service.

The premise

If being near transport helps a venue anywhere, it should help in inner Melbourne

Let's start with the place that should benefit most. Each region on the map is an SA2, a neighbourhood of a few thousand people, and within 400 m means a venue is roughly a five-minute walk from its nearest stop. Across inner Melbourne the map is almost uniformly dark. In the typical neighbourhood about nine in ten venues sit within that five-minute walk. This is not a few inner-city strips happening to sit near a bus/tram stop but whole hospitality districts are wrapped around this transport mesh.

And inner Melbourne is in a class of its own. The chart on the right lines up every area by the share of its venues near a stop. Metro neighbourhoods crowd up against 100%, while regional Victoria trails nearly thirty points behind. So if being close to public transport helps a venue at all, this is where the help should show up. The next section puts that expectation to the test.

Close but no Cigar

Survival rates of Metro vs Regional Venues

If proximity paid off anywhere, it would be inner Melbourne. To test it, the licence list is published every month, so we take every venue licensed in July 2022 and follow it forward. Is the same venue still licensed one year later? Two? Three? (A licence that vanishes is our stand-in for a venue that closed.) The first chart splits that cohort by region, and the surprise is the gap between the lines: regional Victoria, with far less transport access, out-survives metro at every distance band and every horizon. The places with the most access fare the worst.

Read this as an association, not a cause. Metro and regional are different markets in many ways beyond transport, so the gap can't be pinned on access alone; the firmer signal is the flat line within each region. (A cancelled licence can also mean a sale or rename, so survival here is a proxy.)

Survival rates do fall over time — about 84% of venues opened in 2022 were still trading after one year, dropping to around 76% after three. But use the Survival horizon selector to step through those years and one thing never changes: the line stays flat. Venues right beside a stop survive at the same rate as those a long walk away. That pattern holds whether you compare metro regions against regional ones, or venues near and far within the same region. More transport access simply doesn't translate into better survival odds. The rest of this section tests the same finding from different angles — a suburb-level scatter, a growth measure, and two maps. The answer doesn't change.

One possible reason

Fewer people are riding to the door

§3 showed the venues with the most transport access didn't last better; if anything, worse. It can't say why, but one trend that is continuously changing is how Melburnians actually travel.

Public-transport patronage has not recovered to pre-pandemic levels. Averaged across 2025, train trips are running at about 75% of the 2018–19 baseline, tram at 76%, and bus at 91%: roughly 21% fewer metropolitan PT trips per month overall (≈37 million now vs ≈47 million then). The COVID trough in August 2020 was 4.9 million; recovery has been steady but partial.

Some of that travel has shifted to rideshare. Roy Morgan's quarterly tracker (June 2025) puts 7.4 million Australians aged 14+ using Uber in the March 2025 quarter (32.3% of the 14+ population), and 40.6% above the pre-pandemic peak of 5.27 million. Taxi use has stagnated around 4.2 million. None of this proves rideshare drained PT-near venues — those figures are national, not Melbourne, and a usage rate, not trip counts, but the trend is consistent with the §3 finding: the historical "tram-near venue = guaranteed foot traffic" assumption is weaker now than it was a decade ago.

But there is a simpler observation underneath all this: public transport was never how most people reach a venue. Victoria's travel survey (VISTA) records how people actually travelled to pubs, bars, restaurants, cafes and clubs, and across its two most recent waves only about 5% of those trips arrived by public transport. Two in three came by car; a quarter came on foot. If barely a twentieth of venue visits use a stop, then sitting near one was always the baseline, not the edge, which is just what the §3 bust shows.

And travel habits are only one of several plausible reasons. Hospitality is already so concentrated near public transport (78% of venues within 400 m statewide) that proximity is a near-universal condition rather than a distinguishing advantage; rents, lease length and venue type plausibly matter more than the walk to a tram so the assumption of PT helping a venue holds a lot less weight than you would initially think.

Patronage figures count PTV trips in Melbourne (Department of Transport & Planning Victoria, CC BY 4.0). The mode mix is from VISTA (the same department's travel survey): a sample of trips, so the shares are weighted estimates pooled across 2022–23 and 2023–24, and some bars (rideshare especially) rest on only a handful of surveyed trips.
