Close without the Cigar

Victoria's hospitality crowds in tight against public transport. You'd expect that closeness to pay off. It doesn't keep a venue's doors open.

Max Fergie · FIT2179 Data Visualisation 2 · May 2026

Setup

Victoria's venues huddle against the transport network

Where the state drinks, dines and gathers — and how short the walk is to a stop. Across all of Victoria, then zoomed in on Melbourne, the venues settle exactly where the network is densest. It's hard not to assume that closeness pays off: a tram at the door should mean passing trade, a steady flow of people who never had to find a park. Hold onto that assumption — the rest of the page puts it to the test.

One clarification first. Throughout, near a stop means proximity — how far you'd walk to board — not how often a service actually runs. Colour on these maps is distance, not timetable.

The premise

The closeness is real — and it's everywhere

This isn't a handful of inner-city strips. Across the metropolitan map almost every venue sits within a short walk of a stop, and that closeness barely shifts with wealth. Each region below is an SA2; within 400 m means a venue is roughly a five-minute walk from its nearest stop.

Split metro from regional and the apparent income gradient dissolves: inside Melbourne the most and least advantaged neighbourhoods are about equally close to transport — flat across every decile. (Pool the two regions together and a false wealth gradient appears; that's a Simpson's paradox, and it's a trap, not the story.) So you can't duck what's coming by assuming most venues aren't really near a stop. They are.

Close without the cigar

All that closeness — and it doesn't keep the doors open

So does sitting near a stop actually help a venue last? We can test it. The licence list is published monthly, so we take every venue licensed in one July and ask a blunt question two years on: was it still licensed two Julys later? A licence that vanishes is our stand-in for a venue that closed. The chart starts on the July 2023 cohort; use the cohort year selector beneath it to step through the other cohorts and see whether the pattern holds from one year to the next.

Here is the bust. Being near a stop gives a venue no survival advantage. Survival is high almost everywhere — 87 to 93 per cent — and proximity doesn't lift it. Inside Melbourne the venues packed closest to transport actually closed a touch more often (87.6% within 400 m vs 90.8% further out), while regional venues last just as well far from a stop. If closeness paid off, the access–survival line would tilt upward; the faint dashed line on the middle chart shows what even a modest payoff would look like. The real line is flat.

Why this is, we can only speculate (see below). And a cancelled licence can mean a sale, a rename or a move, not only a failure — so survival here is a proxy and the relationship is an association, never proof that proximity does or doesn't cause a venue to last. Distance, too, is measured against today's stops for every cohort, so it is exact for recent venues and only approximate the further back a cohort sits. What the data shows is firm; the reasons are not.

One possible reason

Fewer people are riding to the door

§3 showed proximity doesn't predict survival. It can't say why — but one thing has plainly changed since most of these venues opened: how Melburnians actually travel.

Public-transport patronage has not recovered to pre-pandemic levels. Averaged across 2025, train trips are running at about 75% of the 2018–19 baseline, tram at 76%, and bus at 91% — roughly 21% fewer metropolitan PT trips per month overall (≈37 million now vs ≈47 million then). The COVID trough in August 2020 was 4.9 million; recovery has been steady but partial.

Some of that travel has shifted to rideshare. Roy Morgan's quarterly tracker (June 2025) puts 7.4 million Australians aged 14+ using Uber in the March 2025 quarter — 32.3% of the 14+ population, and 40.6% above the pre-pandemic peak of 5.27 million. Taxi use has stagnated at 4.2 million. None of this proves rideshare drained PT-near venues — those figures are national, not Melbourne, and a usage rate, not trip counts — but the trend is consistent with the §3 finding: the historical "tram-near venue = guaranteed foot traffic" assumption is weaker now than it was a decade ago.

And rideshare is only one of several plausible reasons. Hospitality is already so concentrated near public transport (78% within 400 m statewide) that being near a stop is the baseline, not an advantage; rents, lease length and venue type plausibly matter more than the walk to a tram.

Patronage figures count PTV trips in Melbourne (Department of Transport & Planning Victoria, CC BY 4.0). The Uber/taxi figures count the share of Australians 14+ who used the service in a quarter (Roy Morgan, June 2025). Different units; placed beside each other, never compared on a single axis. Context, not causation.
